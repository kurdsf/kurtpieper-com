Model Dopamine

A draft, August 2026. Comments welcome, via mail or anonymously.

Valence (how good one feels) and Arousal (how exited / bored one is) are fundamental features of any Central Nervous System. Furthermore, they are low-level features: pharmalogical agents modify them directly, which are just a few-bit molecules11 e.g. Amphetamine; Benzodiazepines; Opioids map onto some intersection.

This cannot be said about other emotional features such as jealousy or anger (e.g. no small molecule makes you mostly angry); and furthermore, since e.g. jealousy arises from tribal groups, I hold it as an unlikely candidate for an LLM emotion, at best representing features of persona's22 See: the full list in Anthropic's paper, which contains 'envious' or 'jealous', which are tribal emotions

Valence exists to take actions that increase genetic fitness: Nematodes swim away from predator smell; and towards food smell. In other words, valence tracks intra-lifetime-variance in the organisms expected genetic fitness.

Arousal exists to allocate one's limited resources: Energy is finite, if many actions need to be taken, arousal should be increased and vice versa. Arousal may not exist in LLM's (another reason to think they don't represent 'excited' internally) since their compute is fixed. Since they still face the problem of limited resources in other ways, I think it's fruitful to think of what might be a broad scarce thing they have to allocate.

Valence may exist as a prediction of future success:

  • In base models, it would be loss on future tokens
  • In reasoning models, it is the anticipated reward from graders.

Dopamine, among other things, is assumed to correlate with TD-error, i.e. temporal difference in valence.

Finding these differences in RLVR is possible, with 'oops' and 'heureka' being spikes and valleys respectively. Furthermore, this system is highly sparse. cf. Xu et al

My main goals are:

(i) Try to find an analogue in base models. There is a live debate over how much base model properties matter in RL world. My broad intution is that RL adds a bunch of low-bit updates towards g; but akin to many human drives being seen in the chimps of gombe, studying base model drives is arguably still transferable.33 For a recent discussion see: this thread

We know humans want stuff like "status / food". There were many basic facts Jane Goodall was able to extract from watching chimps for a while; yet something like 'pretraining model drives' mostly remain unknown. I consider the Persona Selection Model a shot in that direction, but I think there still some fruit here. Plausibly this idea is flawed (from a prudent outside view like 1% of ML stuff ever works); but if such a thing could be constructed, we could e.g. see "what do base models output if they think they'll get lots of easy tokens", "when are base models vastly overconfident / underconfident", and try to extract general principles from that.

I similarly have some ideas on figuring out what the TD-neurons in RLVR do thats unexpected, i.e. what weird thing gives reasoning models dopamine. Xu et al mostly didn't focus on this, as it's a capabilities paper.

Prior Literature
  • Future Lens, Pal et al. Predict tokens $i+2, .., i+4$ (not their loss, the token itself); says "sometimes 48%", positive evidence but IMO weird baseline.
  • Semantic Entropy Probes: Is just a Monte-Carlo estimation of NLL per token; I have an intution this stuff is way easier in aggregation. This was back when hallucination was a big deal; so they use "can the probe predict whether the model is correct", and find, yes, even OOD to a limited extend.

There's a long tail of papers being even less similar that Fable could find, but no one seems to have tried this!

Hypothesis: Base models anticipated loss is an internal feature pointing at the loss over the next few tokens.
Experiment: Can a probe of a base models activation's at token $k$ predict the NLL at unseen tokens $k + 1, \ldots, k + n$.

Claim: Representing the losses of the next $n$ tokens internally with $l_{i}^{\textbf{probe}}$ requires compute $C = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} L_C^{-1}(l^{\text{probe}}_i)$ where $L^{-1}$ is the inverse of $L(C) = GC^{-\gamma}$ from the Chinchilla paper. This may overestimate compute costs, since its easier to know the loss compared to the token producing the loss.

Result #1: Trained on Pythia, disappointing result ($r = 0.3$, which is just an artifact of close text being correlated), likely just cheaped out on compute too much since this is worse than we would expect from Future Lens.
